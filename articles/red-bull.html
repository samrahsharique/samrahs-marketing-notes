The Setup

Every brand strategy textbook tells you to find your USP. Red Bull did something far more dangerous — it invented its own category, and then convinced the world that the category was a lifestyle.

Let me start with a fact that still blows my mind. Red Bull spends roughly one-third of its annual revenue on marketing. Not a normal 10–15%. One third. For a company that crossed $10 billion in sales, that's a staggering number. But when you understand what Red Bull actually is — not an energy drink company, but a full-blown media, sports, and storytelling empire that happens to sell a can — you start to realise the money isn't "marketing spend." It's the product.

I want to walk you through the entire Red Bull arc: where it came from, the genius of its early brand-building, the creation of one of the most recognisable slogans in history, the birth of Red Bull Media House, and the series of record-shattering stunts that turned a beverage brand into a symbol of human ambition. I want to pull back the curtain and show you the strategic logic underneath each move — because once you see it, you can't unsee it.

Chapter 01

The Thai tonic that changed everything

The year is 1982. The place: Bangkok. Dietrich Mateschitz — a 38-year-old Austrian marketing executive working for a German toothpaste company called Blendax — lands in Thailand exhausted from jet lag. A local hands him a small glass bottle of something called Krating Daeng. The jet lag vanishes. He is stunned.

But here's what most people miss about this moment. Mateschitz wasn't just impressed by the drink. He was impressed by the idea of the drink. Krating Daeng — literally "Red Gaur" (a wild bovine) in Thai — had been created in 1976 by Chaleo Yoovidhya, a self-made son of Chinese immigrants who had risen from duck farming to pharmaceuticals. Chaleo had developed the tonic for Thailand's working class: truck drivers, factory workers, farmers who needed to stay awake through punishing 16-hour shifts. It worked through a potent blend of caffeine, taurine, B vitamins, and sugar.

What Mateschitz recognised was that no such product existed in the West — and more importantly, the concept of "functional energy" had never been positioned as a premium aspiration for the ambitious and adventurous. That gap was his entire business case.

Strategic Insight

Mateschitz didn't just import a product. He imported a concept and built an entirely new category around it. The drink was the vehicle. The idea — that you could purchase energy, performance, and ambition — was the real product. This is Blue Ocean Strategy before the term was even coined.

In 1984, Mateschitz struck a deal with Chaleo: each would invest $500,000, take a 49% stake in a new company — Red Bull GmbH — with the remaining 2% going to Chaleo's son. Mateschitz would run the company. Then came three years of grueling formula refinement, regulatory battles, and near-bankruptcy. The European licensing process cost him over a million dollars before a single can was sold. He didn't give up.

The key adaptations for the Western market were deliberate and strategic. The drink was carbonated to match European taste preferences. The packaging was moved to the now-iconic slim, blue-and-silver aluminium can — a form that stood apart from every soft drink on the shelf. The price was set significantly higher than alternatives. This wasn't a cost-recovery decision. It was a positioning decision. Expensive things feel effective. Mateschitz knew this.

On April 1, 1987, Red Bull launched in Austria. The response was initially cold. Retailers didn't know where to put it. Was it a medicine? A soft drink? A health tonic? Consumers found the taste strange. That confusion, in hindsight, was actually a brand opportunity — it signalled that something genuinely new had arrived.

Chapter 02

The anti-brand that built the brand

Here's where Mateschitz made decisions that most brand managers — trained on conventional wisdom — would never have made. With barely any budget and a product nobody understood, he chose to do the exact opposite of what traditional marketing dictated.

No primetime TV spots. No billboard campaigns. No celebrity endorsements. No press releases. Instead, he targeted the one group whose opinion spreads fastest and most authentically: university students. Red Bull sent student brand ambassadors — the original "Wings Team" — onto campuses with free cans. They showed up at libraries during exam week, at dormitory parties at midnight, at sports pitches on Friday afternoons. They gave the product to people who actually needed it, in contexts where it proved itself.

The second move was even more counterintuitive. Red Bull deliberately placed empty cans in clubs, bars, and trendy venues. The visual cue was clear: everyone's drinking it. This manufactured social proof before the concept of social proof had a marketing textbook. Scarcity signals, popularity signals, exclusivity — all engineered without a single paid placement.

Strategic Insight

Red Bull understood something that most brands still don't: people don't adopt products, they adopt identities. By placing cans in the right places — ski resorts, university bars, motorsport circuits — they didn't just reach target consumers, they defined who the Red Bull consumer was. The placement was the positioning.

Mateschitz also made an early bet on extreme sports — not because they had a big audience, but because they had an intensely devoted one. In the early 1990s, when BMX, skateboarding, cliff diving, and snowboarding were still considered fringe, Red Bull was showing up, sponsoring athletes, putting logos on helmets and boards. They weren't buying reach. They were buying cultural authenticity.

The brand's seeding strategy — getting product into the right hands, right environments — drove word-of-mouth long before social media gave it a formal name. When Red Bull expanded into Hungary, Germany, and the UK in the early 1990s, then stormed the US market in 1997 (capturing an estimated 75% of the then-nascent energy drink market within a year), it was on the back of this grassroots, tribal approach to brand-building.

There was also something quietly brilliant about how Red Bull handled controversy. When rumours spread that the drink contained bull hormones, or even cocaine, Mateschitz let them run. He understood that controversy, when it orbits a product, creates fascination. When several countries threatened to ban Red Bull, each episode generated millions in free press. The brand leaned into danger and edge rather than running from it. That took nerve.

Chapter 03

The five words that built a billion-dollar mythology

"Red Bull gives you wings."

Five words. One of the most valuable pieces of intellectual property in the history of consumer brands. Let's dissect why this slogan is so strategically perfect — because it's not an accident.

The slogan emerged from Red Bull's early collaboration with the German advertising agency Kastner & Partners, launched in its full form in the early 1990s and cemented globally through its now-famous animated campaigns starting in 1992. The German original — "Red Bull Verleiht Flügel" — translates literally to "Red Bull grants wings." Every word in that phrase was doing strategic work.

Why "wings" was genius, not whimsy

First, notice what the slogan does not say. It does not say "Red Bull gives you energy." It does not say "Red Bull improves focus." It does not make a medical or nutritional claim. It makes a metaphorical, aspirational, legally defensible promise about transformation. Wings is not a function. Wings is a feeling. Wings is an identity. You don't drink Red Bull for the caffeine — you drink it to feel like someone who could do anything.

Second, the slogan is a call to action embedded in a product promise. "Gives you wings" implies that wings are something you go do something with. It creates agency. It pushes you toward ambition, action, movement. This is enormously different from slogans like "Just Do It" (which tells you what to do) or "Think Different" (which tells you who to be). Red Bull says: here is the fuel, now fly.

"They weren't making a medical claim. They were selling a feeling — an aspiration. That is the difference between a commodity and a brand."

— On the strategic genius of "Red Bull Gives You Wings"

The cartoon ads: simplicity as strategy

Starting with the now-legendary Leonardo da Vinci cartoon in 1992, Red Bull built an animated campaign that ran for decades with a formula so consistent it became its own visual language. The format: a simple, hand-drawn character in a low-energy, frustrating situation. They drink Red Bull. Wings sprout. Problem solved.

The humour is dry. The drawing style is deliberately crude. The situations are universal — a tired office worker, a sleepy student, a struggling athlete. And here lies the brilliance: by using simple, internationally legible animation rather than live action, Red Bull built a campaign that crossed every language and cultural barrier effortlessly. The jokes landed in Tokyo as well as Texas. The brand spent decades in TV with ads that were, essentially, charming doodles — while every competitor was spending enormous budgets on polished, expensive live-action spots.

What nobody talks about enough: the cartoon format also deliberately kept the product peripheral. The story is never about the drink. It's always about the human. Red Bull understood that people connect with people, not products. The can is just the catalyst.

Strategic Insight

The crude animation style was not a budget constraint. It was a strategic signal. "We're not a polished corporate brand" — we're scrappy, irreverent, a little weird. Just like extreme sports. Just like the young people we're targeting. The aesthetic was the positioning.

Chapter 04

The moment Red Bull stopped being a beverage company

By the mid-2000s, Red Bull had already established itself as the world's dominant energy drink. It had infiltrated Formula One, sponsored hundreds of extreme athletes, and put its logo on events from cliff diving to snowboarding to air races. But Mateschitz saw something the industry didn't: the brand's greatest asset wasn't the product. It was the content the brand generated.

In 2007, Red Bull officially founded Red Bull Media House — a full-scale, independent media company headquartered in Salzburg, Austria, with a North American base in Santa Monica. This was not a marketing department rebranding itself. This was a genuine, standalone media production and distribution company with a separate mandate.

Think about what this meant strategically. Most brands in 2007 were asking: how do we buy media attention? Red Bull asked: how do we own media? Instead of paying TV networks to rent their audiences' attention for 30 seconds, Red Bull decided to build the programming that audiences would seek out voluntarily.

Strategic Insight

Red Bull Media House inverted the entire logic of advertising spend. Traditional brands are interruptions inside someone else's content. Red Bull became the content itself. The audience wasn't targeted — they were earned. That is a fundamentally different relationship between brand and consumer.

What Red Bull Media House built was extraordinary in scale and ambition. Red Bull TV became a streaming platform offering extreme sports, music, and adventure documentaries. The Red Bulletin — launched in 2005 — became a lifestyle magazine distributed globally with a readership exceeding 7 million, covering everything from adventure and culture to innovation and music. The division produced over 100 full-length feature films and documentaries, many receiving theatrical releases and critical acclaim — including The Art of Flight (2011), a snowboarding film that became a commercial hit in action sports circles, and The Fourth Phase (2016).

By the time Red Bull Media House had matured, the company operated more than 900 digital domains in 36 languages, employed over 400 people globally in media production alone, and had one of the largest brand-run YouTube channels in the world with over 21 million subscribers. But the number that matters most to a marketing professional: this content almost never explicitly advertises the drink. It tells stories of athletes. It documents human achievement. It creates culture. Red Bull's logo is present — on the athletes' suits, on the event banners, on the capsule — but the story is always about the human being at the centre of it.

This is the principle that separates Red Bull's content strategy from every imitator: the brand earns the right to be in the story by making the story worth telling.

"Red Bull doesn't interrupt your entertainment.
Red Bull is your entertainment.
That's not marketing.
That's a media company."

Chapter 05

When the slogan became literal

Red Bull's stunt portfolio is genuinely unlike anything in the history of brand marketing. These are not PR stunts in the conventional sense — manufactured moments with a press release attached. They are multi-year, multi-million dollar productions that happen to also be branded. Each one was designed to be the most remarkable version of a thing ever attempted. Each one was designed to make the slogan — "Red Bull gives you wings" — feel literally, viscerally, undeniably true.

The Crown Jewel — October 14, 2012

Red Bull Stratos: The Space Jump

The single greatest piece of branded content ever produced. Felix Baumgartner — Austrian paratrooper, BASE jumper, and professional daredevil — stepped out of a capsule in the stratosphere 39 kilometres above the New Mexico desert. He was wearing a custom pressure suit. The capsule had been lifted there by a helium balloon the size of a skyscraper. What happened next was watched live by 8.3 million people on YouTube — the largest live stream audience in history at that point.

Baumgartner fell for 4 minutes and 19 seconds. At peak velocity, he reached 1,357.64 km/h — Mach 1.25 — breaking the sound barrier with his body. He shattered three world records: highest manned balloon flight, fastest freefall speed, and highest freefall parachute jump at 38,969 metres (127,851 feet). He was the first human being to break the sound barrier without any engine power.

The project had been in planning since 2005 — a seven-year investment. The total budget exceeded $30 million. In return: a 7% spike in Red Bull sales, a $1.6 billion revenue boost, billions of media impressions globally, and a piece of branded content that is still being studied, referenced, and taught in marketing programmes worldwide more than a decade later.

But the most important number isn't any of those. It's this: not once during the entire broadcast was there a "Buy Red Bull now" message. The product was invisible. The idea of the product — that Red Bull makes you capable of impossible things — was everywhere.

September 25, 2025 — Porto Alegre, Brazil

Red Bull Building Drop: Skateboarding a skyscraper

For over 30 years, residents of Porto Alegre had looked at the curved façade of the 22-story Centro Administrativo Fernando Ferrari (CAFF) building and thought it looked like a skate ramp. Red Bull made the joke real.

Six-time vert world champion Sandro Dias — 50 years old, still at the peak of his sport — dropped from 70 metres above the ground down a custom-built plywood overlay on the building's face, reaching speeds of 103.8 km/h (64 mph) on a standard skateboard. The stunt required months of preparation including training with a 40-kilogram weighted vest to simulate the 3.9 g-forces he would experience. He set two Guinness World Records: tallest drop into a temporary quarter pipe, and fastest speed on a temporary quarter pipe. His final descent from full height had never been practiced in full before the live attempt. It lasted eight seconds.

The brand also pledged to fund two public skateparks in the region — a rare moment of tangible community legacy attached to a marketing event.

The Red Bull stunts roster goes on and on. Red Bull Rampage — the annual freeride mountain biking competition in the Utah desert, where riders launch off natural cliffs onto handbuilt jumps. Red Bull Cliff Diving World Series — where athletes hurl themselves off platforms 27 metres above the ocean in locations from Portugal to Mostar to Polignano. Red Bull Air Race — aerobatic pilots navigating inflatable pylons at 370 km/h metres above the water. Robbie Maddison's 2008 New Year's Eve backflip over the Arc de Triomphe replica in Las Vegas. The list is genuinely inexhaustible.

Each event follows the same architecture: identify a human being operating at the absolute limit of what is physically possible in a particular domain. Remove every barrier between that human and their most ambitious attempt. Document everything. Release the content to the world. Never put the drink front and centre. Let the idea — wings — speak for itself.

Chapter 06

The timeline of an empire

1976
Krating Daeng is born

Chaleo Yoovidhya creates a functional tonic for Thai truck drivers and factory workers. The DNA of Red Bull — caffeine, taurine, B vitamins — exists in glass bottles sold at roadside stalls.

1982 – 1984
The fateful encounter

Mateschitz discovers Krating Daeng on a Bangkok business trip. A $500,000 each investment seals a 49/49 partnership. Three years of formula refinement, regulatory battles, and near-bankruptcy begin.

1987
Red Bull launches in Austria

The slim silver can, the new category, and the premium price point hit Austrian shelves. Initial reception is confused. Mateschitz begins seeding at ski resorts and university campuses.

1992
The first cartoon ad airs

The Leonardo da Vinci spot marks the beginning of the hand-drawn animation campaign that will run for decades. The "Gives You Wings" visual language is born — simple, dry, universal.

1995 – 2000s
Extreme sports ownership begins

Red Bull partners with Sauber in Formula One (its first F1 appearance), expands into BMX, skateboarding, snowboarding, cliff diving. The brand becomes inseparable from the culture of extreme sports — not a sponsor, a constituent.

1997
The US conquest

Red Bull enters the American market and within a year captures an estimated 75% of the nascent energy drink category. The grassroots seeding model scales globally.

2004 – 2005
Red Bull owns F1 and football

Red Bull Racing enters Formula One as a constructor. Red Bull acquires football clubs in New York (MetroStars, rebranded New York Red Bulls) and Salzburg. The brand isn't just visible in sport — it owns sport.

2007
Red Bull Media House is founded

The single most consequential structural decision in the brand's history. Red Bull officially becomes a media company. Red Bull TV, The Red Bulletin, and a global content production apparatus are born.

2009
RB Leipzig is born

Red Bull acquires the licence of SSV Markranstädt and founds RB Leipzig. By 2016 they reach the Bundesliga. By 2020 they reach the Champions League semi-finals. The brand now competes at the highest level in three major sports.

2012
Stratos — the space jump

Felix Baumgartner jumps from 39km above Earth, breaks the sound barrier with his body, and 8.3 million people watch live. The single greatest branded content event in marketing history.

2025
The Building Drop

Sandro Dias turns a 22-storey government office in Brazil into the world's largest skate ramp. At 50 years old, he drops 70 metres at 103 km/h. Two Guinness World Records. The playbook never changes.

Chapter 07

The five principles that built the empire

Across four decades of brand-building, Red Bull's strategy can be distilled into five recurring principles. These are the things that never changed even as everything around them evolved.

The Red Bull Brand Architecture
01

Sell the identity, not the ingredient

Red Bull never competed on taste, nutrition, or price-value ratios. It competed on who you are when you drink it. The drink was the accessory. The identity — adventurous, ambitious, physically capable, boundary-pushing — was the actual product. This is why people who don't even like the taste of Red Bull still buy it.

02

Own the culture before it goes mainstream

Red Bull invested in BMX, skateboarding, snowboarding, and cliff diving when those sports had tiny audiences. When they exploded into mainstream culture — when skateboarding entered the Olympics, when snowboarding became a global phenomenon — Red Bull was already the defining brand. You cannot buy that authenticity at any price after the fact. The insight: invest in emerging cultures early, own them deeply, and let the culture's growth carry the brand into scale.

03

Be the story, not the sponsor

The difference between Red Bull and conventional sponsors is ownership. Other brands put logos on events they didn't create. Red Bull creates the events. Red Bull Rampage, Red Bull Stratos, Red Bull Flugtag, Red Bull Building Drop — none of these would exist without Red Bull. The brand isn't attached to the story. The brand is the reason the story exists. That's not sponsorship. That's authorship.

04

Treat athletes as protagonists, not endorsers

Red Bull doesn't hire athletes to appear in advertisements. It builds careers, creates platforms, and documents journeys. Felix Baumgartner wasn't "the Red Bull skydiver" — he was a daredevil with a seven-year dream, and Red Bull made it possible. Sandro Dias had a 13-year-old vision of skating the CAFF building. Red Bull built the ramp. The athlete's story is always the story. The brand's logo is in the corner.

05

Never change the slogan, never stop proving it

For nearly four decades, the core promise has been the same: wings. What shifts is the proof. Each stunt, each event, each athlete story is fresh evidence for the same ancient claim. This is how you build a brand myth — not by changing your story, but by finding newer, bolder, more extraordinary ways to tell it. Consistency of promise. Perpetual escalation of proof.

Final Thought

What Red Bull actually teaches us

The deepest lesson from Red Bull isn't about extreme sports or clever slogans. It's about the fundamental question every brand must answer: what are you actually selling?

Red Bull answered that question with unusual clarity. We are not selling caffeine. We are not selling flavour. We are not selling a moment of hydration. We are selling the belief that human potential has no ceiling. That extraordinary things are possible. That somewhere in the world right now, a person is doing something that has never been done before — and we are the ones who made it possible.

That is not a product claim. That is a worldview. And when a brand manages to embed its identity within a compelling worldview — one that its audience genuinely wants to inhabit — it stops competing in a category and starts defining a culture. It stops being a choice and starts being a symbol.

Red Bull went from a working-class Thai tonic in a glass bottle to the most audacious media and sports empire on earth without ever changing a single word of its promise. "Red Bull gives you wings." They just kept finding new ways to make you believe it.

"From a working-class Thai tonic to the edge of space in 25 years. Not by changing the drink. By never, ever stopping to prove what it stands for."

— Samrah Sharique, Samrah's Marketing Notes

For every brand professional reading this: the question Red Bull forces you to ask yourself is not "what is our marketing strategy?" The question is: "what do we make possible?" The answer to that question — if you can find one that is genuinely true, genuinely ambitious, and genuinely felt — is the foundation of everything.
