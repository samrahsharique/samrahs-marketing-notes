The case at a glance

The bag with the nail clipper

In July 2025, a scuffed black leather bag went under the hammer at Sotheby's in Paris. It had stickers stuck on it, visible wear, and a small nail clipper still attached. After a bidding war of more than ten minutes, it sold for $10.1 million, the most valuable handbag ever sold at auction. gulfnewsCBS News

This was Jane Birkin's own bag, the prototype. Someone paid the price of a Manhattan apartment for a used, slightly grubby handbag.

If you can explain why, you can pass most of a brand management exam. If you can also explain why Hermès lets that happen without capturing a cent of it, you'll get top marks.

This case tries to give you that explanation.

01
The numbers
Numbers first

Everything else in the case is an attempt to explain them.

Numbers first, because everything else in the case is an attempt to explain them.

  • Scale. Full-year 2025 revenue was €16,002 million, with recurring operating income of €6,569 million, a 41.0% margin. hermes
  • Momentum. In H1 2026, revenue reached €8.2 billion, up 6% at constant exchange rates, with a 41.0% recurring operating margin. hermes
  • Gross margin. Gross margin improved to 71.1% from 70.7% a year earlier. That is nearly three-quarters of every euro left after making the product. investing
  • The flagship. Leather goods and saddlery brought in €1.9 billion in Q2, up 8.4%. That is about 46% of the quarter's sales, by my arithmetic. cpp-luxury
  • The blemish. Perfume and beauty fell more than 10% to €107 million, and the group's shares dropped about 8% in premarket trading after the results, nearing their 52-week low. cpp-luxuryinvesting

Keep that last point in mind. It matters at the end.

For context, a margin above 40% makes Hermès look more like a software company than a leather-goods maker. The margin also isn't a fluke. The company has stayed above 40% since 2021, though its 2023 peak was 44.0%. The rest of this case explains how a company that hand-stitches bags in French workshops earns software-style margins. investing

02
Evolution
A brand that pivoted without changing its mind

Textbooks love to tell brand stories as a straight line.

Hermès is more interesting because it has pivoted repeatedly while keeping one core competence.

The aha moment. Hermès never really left saddlery. A Birkin is a saddle-stitched, hand-finished leather object made for people who ride. Only the user changed. In academic terms, Hermès extends by competence fit (Prahalad & Hamel's core competence) rather than by category fit. It doesn't ask "what else do rich people buy?" It asks "what else can our hands make at this level?"

That is why the group organizes itself around 16 métiers, meaning crafts, rather than product categories. cpp-luxury

03
Identity
Kapferer's Prism applied

Hermès is unusually coherent on all six facets.

Jean-Noël Kapferer's Brand Identity Prism describes a brand through six facets. Hermès is unusually coherent on all of them.

  • Physique (the tangible signature): the orange box, the saddle stitch, the H buckle, the carriage-and-horse emblem, and the silhouettes of the Kelly and Birkin.
  • Personality: witty, playful, understated. Hermès is oddly humorous for a luxury brand, and the yearly creative theme is a classic example.
  • Culture: French craft, equestrian heritage, family ownership, and time as a value (waiting, patina, repair).
  • Relationship: earned. Access comes through a sales associate who knows you, not through a click.
  • Reflection (how the brand sees its typical user): someone discreet, knowledgeable, and unhurried.
  • Self-image (how the buyer feels using it): "I'm someone who knows." This is quiet luxury before the term went viral.
Exam tip

The strength of the Prism lies in consistency across facets. Hermès scores well because the physique (visible craft), the culture (artisan), and the relationship (earned access) all say the same thing. When a brand's facets contradict each other, that is the diagnostic language for identity drift, for example a heritage brand chasing hype drops.

04
Equity
Keller's pyramid

And what happens at the summit.

Use Keller's Customer-Based Brand Equity (CBBE) pyramid to structure any equity answer:

  1. Salience: the orange box and H logo are recognizable without a name on them.
  2. Performance: durability, repairability, and material quality.
  3. Imagery: aristocratic, equestrian, discreet.
  4. Judgments: quality, credibility, and superiority are near-automatic.
  5. Feelings: pride, belonging, and the thrill of being "chosen."
  6. Resonance: behavioral loyalty, attachment, community, and active engagement. Collectors trade, restore, and curate.

The twist. Hermès has built resonance so strong that it created a secondary market, the auction houses and resellers, that captures a large part of the brand's economic value. Sotheby's resells Birkins for between $5,000 and $220,000, and the previous auction record for a Birkin, a diamond Himalaya version, was over $450,000 in 2022. CBS News1stdibs

We can turn that into a diagnostic. Define the resale premium:

R = (P_secondary − P_retail) / P_retail

A persistently positive R means demand exceeds supply at the listed price, and the size of R is a market-revealed measure of brand desirability. Hermès could raise retail prices to close the gap but doesn't fully do so. The brand is effectively investing its pricing headroom in desirability. Economists would call the gap unpriced rent. Hermès treats it as a marketing budget it never has to report.

The Aha Moment
Hermès lets the secondary market prove what the brand can make people want.
05
The engine
Scarcity, price, and allocation

Veblen, snobs, and commodity theory.

Three theories together explain why lower supply increases desire:

  • Veblen effect: for status goods, higher price can raise demand because price signals exclusivity.
  • Snob effect (Leibenstein): demand falls when too many others own the item. This is the constraint the Hermès model is built to respect.
  • Commodity theory (Brock) and psychological reactance: people value what is hard to get and push harder when access is denied.

Patrick Thomas once summed up the tension as a paradox: the more desirable a brand becomes, the more it sells, but the more it sells, the less desirable it becomes. Hermès's job is to keep sliding along that knife edge. martinroll

Kapferer's "anti-laws" of luxury

Kapferer and Bastien argue that luxury strategy inverts classical marketing. Hermès is the poster child for several of the anti-laws:

  • Don't pander to customers' every wish.
  • Don't respond mechanically to rising demand.
  • Keep non-enthusiasts out.
  • Communicate to a wider audience than the one you sell to, so the dream stays big while the buyer pool stays small.

Real scarcity versus manufactured scarcity

Many brands fake scarcity with "limited drops." Hermès scarcity is structural. Craft is slow, and Axel Dumas says the ceiling on leather goods is the number of trained artisans and the supply of hides at a quality Hermès accepts. He told analysts he would sell more leather if he produced more, but that he won't produce below the required level of quality. the-silent-luxury

Look at the operations as a brand decision. Hermès has been opening about one workshop a year. In April 2026 it opened its 25th leather goods workshop, at Loupes near Bordeaux, focused mainly on the Kelly. Every artisan goes through an 18-month training course at the École Hermès des savoir-faire before joining a production line. An executive said the company is not about to pursue productivity gains. Hermès Opens Its 25th Leather Goods Workshop in France, Reinforcing Growth Through Increased Kelly Production +2

Here is a simple growth identity:

g_supply ≈ g_artisans + g_productivity

In most industries, management pushes g_productivity as high as it will go. Hermès deliberately holds it near zero, so supply grows only as fast as it can train people. Supply growth becomes a slow, predictable, brand-protective curve set by the training pipeline, and no quarterly pressure can accelerate it.

Aha moment

At Hermès, the HR department is arguably the brand-protection department.

Allocation as a relationship device

There is no official public waitlist for the Birkin. Allocation runs through purchase history, relationship tenure with a specific sales associate, and demonstrated commitment to the wider Hermès range. ehl

In Rust, Zeithaml & Lemon's Customer Equity framework, this is a way to build relationship equity:

CE = Value equity + Brand equity + Relationship equity

Value equity is the objective quality-for-price judgment, brand equity is the subjective image, and relationship equity is the "stickiness" from the personal bond. Hermès uses scarce products to manufacture relationship equity: you become a client by earning your way in.

06
The lawsuit
The legal system tests the model

Brand strategy meets antitrust.

In 2024, shoppers filed a class action alleging illegal "tying" of Birkin purchases to other Hermès items. The complaint also alleged that associates earn commission on other goods but not on Birkins. NPRNPR

The judge dismissed the claims with prejudice in September 2025. He found that the plaintiffs failed to define a coherent market or show that Hermès had market power or harmed competition, and he wrote that reserving the Birkin for top-paying customers is not, in itself, an antitrust violation. The plaintiffs appealed, and Hermès urged the Ninth Circuit in May 2026 to affirm. Hermès Faces Appeal Over Dismissed Birkin Bag Antitrust Lawsuit +2

The brand-management lesson is that the brand's competitive set is the whole luxury market, not "Birkin-like handbags." A "brand is a monopoly of one" is a fine marketing metaphor but a bad legal argument. Hermès's ability to win rests on being one brand among many alternatives.

07
Architecture
A branded house full of brands

The master brand does the heavy lifting.

Following Aaker & Joachimsthaler, Hermès is a branded house. The master brand "Hermès" sits on everything, while individual products carry strong descriptors: Kelly, Birkin, Constance, Carré.

Because the master brand does the heavy lifting, Hermès can build product icons that function as sub-brands without splitting the equity. Each icon spreads a halo effect over the range. The Birkin's mystique makes a €400 silk scarf feel like an entry ticket rather than a discount item.

That gives a price-tier pyramid: entry (scarves, small accessories, perfume), core (Kelly, Birkin), and apex (exotic skins, Himalaya editions, the $10.1 million Original). The pyramid works only if the entry level feels authentic and the apex stays out of reach. The weaker H1 result for perfume and beauty is a reminder that the entry tier is where extensions are most exposed. It is also where the pull to "democratize" the brand is strongest, and where Hermès's usual scarcity levers are weakest.

08
Governance
The moat under the moat

Governance as brand strategy.

Most brand cases stop at the customer. The best Hermès insight sits in the cap table.

In October 2010, Patrick Thomas got a call from Bernard Arnault, who told him that LVMH had built up a 17 percent stake and would announce it in two hours. LVMH had used equity swaps that allowed it to avoid declaring its stake in legal filings. The family responded structurally: a private holding company that owns 50.2% of Hermès shares and has first right of refusal when a family member wants to sell. This was H51, and the family described it as a commitment to a 20-year lock-up on selling. Luxury Wars: How Hermès Faced Down its Rival +3

The endgame is instructive. A €8 million AMF fine landed on LVMH in 2013, and in 2014 a settlement had LVMH divest its stake. One family member, Nicolas Puech, refused to join the holding, so even the fortress had a crack. The Hermès Billion-Euro Share Vanishing Act: Nicolas Puech's Trust Trap and LVMH's Blockbuster Takeover Battle — BigGo Finance +2

Why is this a brand strategy? Because a brand built on time (slow craft, long training, patient allocation) is fragile in a market that prizes quarterly growth. H51 buys strategic patience. Compare two governance logics:

  • Agency-theory logic: managers maximize near-term shareholder value, so scarcity is at risk whenever the numbers wobble.
  • Stewardship logic: insiders act as long-term custodians, so refusing to produce more than quality allows is a rational choice.
Aha moment

Hermès's shareholder structure is the legal wrapper that makes its scarcity credible. Without it, an activist could argue with a straight face that "you're leaving money on the table by not making more bags."

09
Communication
The "no marketing department" myth

Product and craft come first. Promotion comes second.

You will often read that Hermès has no marketing department and that everyone is responsible for marketing. Take it as identity discourse rather than a literal org chart. Hermès communicates constantly through the yearly theme, windows, events, and heritage storytelling. The claim itself is a brand story: it tells you that product and craft come first, and promotion second. martinroll

The point for an exam is that Hermès shifts spend from paid persuasion to owned experience and product. In Kapferer's language, it communicates to build the dream and lets the product and access carry the sale.

10
Stress tests
Open issues, 2026

A good case leaves questions unresolved.

Here are Hermès's:

  1. Currency and geography. H1 growth was 6% at constant rates but only 2% at current rates, and the press release flagged the Middle East as the one region without growth. Commentary on the results also pointed to China headwinds. The American and Japanese markets are doing the heavy lifting, with the Americas up 13.7% in Q2. Hermès - First Half Results 2026 - Press Release +2
  2. Margin drift. Falling from a 44% peak to 41% is consistent with heavier capacity investment, FX, and taxes. A student should ask whether that is the cost of protecting scarcity or the start of a trend.
  3. Extension limits. Perfume and beauty is the weak spot. Is that a category problem or a brand-fit problem?
  4. Legal exposure. The Ninth Circuit appeal is still live, and allocation opacity will keep attracting scrutiny.
  5. Value capture. The resale market keeps growing in stature. Should Hermès ever capture it, for example through certified pre-owned? Doing so would risk changing what the brand means.
  6. Valuation versus brand. The stock's drop after results shows that brand equity is not the same as market valuation. A brand can be perfect while its share price is repriced on growth expectations.

Three formulas worth memorizing:

  • Price premium = (P_brand − P_reference) / P_reference
  • Customer equity = Value + Brand + Relationship equity
  • Brand value (Interbrand-style) ≈ Σ [brand-attributable earnings × role-of-brand index] discounted at a rate reflecting brand strength

Common traps: confusing exclusivity (who can buy) with scarcity (how much exists), treating "no advertising" as "no communication," and forgetting that the brand's constraint is capacity and training, not demand.

Discussion questions

11. Discussion questions

  1. Hermès forgoes the resale premium. Under what conditions would capturing it destroy more brand equity than it creates?
  2. Is Hermès's "no productivity gains" stance a strategy or a constraint? How would you tell?
  3. Use the Prism to explain why a "quiet luxury" trend helps or hurts a brand that has always been quiet.
  4. Could H51-style governance work for a brand whose core competence isn't craft? Why or why not?
