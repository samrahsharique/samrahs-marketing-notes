The setup

Just Undo It? Nike and the Most Expensive Lesson in Channel Strategy

Start with three numbers:

$35. The price Nike paid a design student for the Swoosh in 1971.

$126 million. First-year sales of Air Jordan, against an internal goal of about $3 million over three years.

$36. Roughly where the stock trades as I write this, near its 52-week low.

This is a case about how a brand that earned the first two numbers arrived at the third. It is also a live case. Nike reports fiscal Q1 2027 results on October 1, 2026, two days from now, and the ending is deliberately left open. Every framework below is applied to a story still being written.

The thesis

Nike is history's best example of a brand built on meaning. It nearly forgot that meaning has to be delivered through products, shelves, and stories, not dashboards.

The case at a glance

1. The case at a glance

Fiscal 2026 (ended May 31, 2026). Full-year revenues were $46.4 billion, flat on a reported basis and down 2 percent currency-neutral.

The channel story. In Q4, wholesale revenues were $6.6 billion, up 1 percent currency-neutral, while NIKE Direct fell 9 percent currency-neutral to $4.1 billion.

The geography story. Q4 showed modest North America growth offset by declines in Greater China (-17%), EMEA (-6%) and Converse.

The margin story. Full-year gross margin was 42.9%, or 40.8% excluding a benefit from tariff recovery. Read the reported number with care.

The cost of repair. Management cited nearly $400 million of severance charges to reposition the business.

The brand-value story. Interbrand's 2025 ranking has Nike's brand value down 25.9% to $33.7 billion, dropping to 23rd place.

The market's verdict. One analyst notes a 42% decline year to date with shares hovering around $36, close to the 52-week low. Another notes Nike was removed from the S&P 100 earlier in September.

Hold on to that last group. Now go back to how the brand was built.

2. Origins: the myth-maker (Holt and McCracken)

the myth-maker (Holt and McCracken)

Nike began in 1964 as Blue Ribbon Sports. Phil Knight and his former track coach Bill Bowerman formed it to sell Onitsuka Tiger sneakers in the US. In 1971 the company cut ties with Onitsuka and became Nike, using a Swoosh logo bought for $35. The name honors the Greek goddess of victory. That already tells you the brand's category: it never sold shoes, it sold winning.

Two theories explain what happened next.

Douglas Holt's cultural branding. Holt argues that iconic brands don't win by better benefits. They win by delivering an identity myth that resolves a tension in society. Nike's myth, sharpened over decades, is democratized athletic heroism: the belief that greatness is available to anyone willing to struggle. Bowerman's line, "if you have a body, you are an athlete," is the brand's mission statement in one sentence.

McCracken's meaning-transfer model. Meaning flows from the culture at large to a celebrity, then from the celebrity to the product, then from the product to the consumer. Michael Jordan is the textbook case. Nike signed him in 1984 to a $2.5 million, five-year deal with a 5% royalty, and Air Jordan generated $126 million in first-year sales against a $3 million three-year goal. Jordan's on-court mythology was moved onto a shoe, and the shoe carried it into bedrooms and streets.

"Just Do It" (1988) was the brand's myth compressed into three words. The first ad, on July 1, 1988, featured Walt Stack, and Wieden credits the slogan to Gary Gilmore's last words, "Let's do it." It is an oddly dark source for a motivational line, which is precisely why it works: it has no product features in it at all. It is pure identity.

Aha: in Holt's terms, Nike's moat was never the waffle sole. Bowerman invented that by pouring rubber into a waffle iron, and competitors can copy soles. Myths are much harder to copy than features.

3. Identity and architecture: Aaker's lens

Aaker's lens

Aaker's identity system distinguishes a core identity (timeless essence) from an extended identity (supporting associations). Nike's core: authentic athletic performance and inspiration. Its extended identity added rebellion, swagger, and cultural relevance. On Aaker's brand-personality dimensions, Nike scores high on excitement (daring, spirited) and competence (reliable, successful).

Architecture. Nike operates a portfolio, not a single brand:

Master brand

Nike

The parent brand and home of the Swoosh. It carries the core promise of authentic athletic performance and lends credibility to everything beneath it.

Sub-brand

Jordan

An athlete-born sub-brand with its own logo (the Jumpman) that behaves almost like an endorsed brand with independent equity.

Separate brand

Converse

A separately positioned brand acquired in 2003. In the recent numbers, Converse has been a drag rather than a driver.

Jordan is the strategic masterstroke of the architecture. It let Nike build a second identity inside the first, one that could serve style and street culture while Nike proper served performance. That flexibility is also where the trouble later crept in.

4. Equity: Keller's pyramid, and where Nike is weakest

Keller's pyramid, and where Nike is weakest

Walking up Keller's Customer-Based Brand Equity pyramid:

01 / Salience

The Swoosh is a textbook distinctive brand asset (Romaniuk and Sharp): recognizable with no name attached.

02 / Performance

Historically strong, but this is where challengers attacked.

03 / Imagery

Athletic, aspirational, urban.

04 / Judgments

Quality, credibility, superiority.

05 / Feelings

Inspiration, belonging, excitement.

06 / Resonance

Deep behavioral loyalty and identification.

The measurement puzzle, and the sharpest insight in this case. Two respected valuation shops give what looks like contradictory verdicts:

Interbrand

Interbrand says the brand's value fell 25.9% to $33.7 billion. Its methodology ranks brands on financial performance, the role of the brand in purchase intent, and competitive strength.

Brand Finance

Brand Finance, meanwhile, named Nike the strongest apparel brand globally, with a Brand Strength Index score of 94.7 out of 100. Its valuation uses the Royalty Relief approach.

These are not in conflict. They measure different things. In stylized form:

Brand strengthBrand strength = how strongly consumers feel and choose (attitudinal).
Brand valueBrand value = PV of earnings attributable to the brand (financial).
Interbrand-styleInterbrand-style: Value ≈ Σ [ (Economic profit)ₜ × (Role-of-brand index) ] ÷ (1 + r_BS)ᵗ, where the discount rate r_BS falls as brand strength rises.
Royalty-reliefRoyalty-relief: Value ≈ PV of [ Forecast revenue × Royalty rate × (1 − tax) ]

Because both value formulas run through revenue and profit forecasts, a brand can stay strong in consumers' minds while its value shrinks if its sales and profit outlook deteriorate. Nike's attitudes are largely intact. Its commercial conversion is what broke. That sets up the next section.

5. Two crises, two very different outcomes

5. Two crises, two very different outcomes

1998: the sweatshop crisis (image repair)

Activists documented conditions in Nike's contract factories from the early 1990s. The scandal is generally dated to a 1991 report by Jeff Ballinger on garment workers at a Nike factory in Indonesia. Nike's first instinct was defensive: the company initially treated the accusations as public relations problems before shifting to a proactive approach after serious damage to its reputation. Knight's 1998 National Press Club speech conceded that the Nike product had become synonymous with abusive labor practices. A few points worth taking away:

This maps onto Benoit's Image Repair Theory (denial → mortification/corrective action).

It shows the flip side of salience: because Nike was the most visible brand, it became the symbol of an industry-wide problem. Strong brands are lightning rods.

Legally, it also produced Kasky v. Nike, a landmark case about whether a company's defense of its practices counts as commercial speech.

Nike's financial slump in 1998 had several causes. Analysts cite the tarnished image, a failure to follow shifting consumer preferences, and the Asian financial crisis. Be careful not to attribute a downturn to a single cause.

2018: "Dream Crazy" (brand activism)

Nike made Colin Kaepernick the face of the 30th anniversary of "Just Do It." The immediate reaction was noisy. Reports say shares dipped roughly 3.2% the morning after, but Edison Trends recorded a 31 percent increase in Nike online orders in the 72 hours after release. The ad won an Emmy for Outstanding Commercial.

The right framework is segmentation and polarization, not "controversy sells." A stylized payoff:

Net brand effect = Σᵢ (segment share × strategic weight × sentiment shift)

The boycott segment (older, lower Nike involvement) mattered less than the core growth segment (younger, urban, higher lifetime value), whose sentiment moved up. Holt would say Nike did not chase a stunt. It aligned with a live cultural tension that its own myth already implied: believing in something is the point of the whole brand.

A caution. Scholars have also criticized the campaign as inconsistent with Nike's own labor history and corporate conduct, so authenticity is a contested word in this case. A strong analysis can note that brand activism creates an authenticity liability: the audience will audit the company's behavior against its stance.

6. The pivot that cracked the moat: "Consumer Direct Acceleration"

6. The pivot that cracked the moat: "Consumer Direct Acceleration"

Core diagnosis

Here is the core strategic error, and it is a channel error, not a brand error.

Under CEO John Donahoe, Nike accelerated its direct-to-consumer model. It left Amazon in 2019 and de-emphasized wholesale partners. It is a seductive idea: capture the retailer's margin, own the customer data, control the story. Look at what actually happened:

Nike relied too heavily on DTC sales during the pandemic and shunned longtime wholesale partners such as Foot Locker, which then filled their walls with other brands.

As customers returned to stores, they bought sneakers from running brands like On and Hoka, and Asics and New Balance for lifestyle wear.

Nike had leaned heavily on high-volume retro sneakers like the Air Force 1 and Dunk, and the market became oversaturated.

Nike's own leadership later admitted the plan added "complexity and inefficiency," and a Wall Street analyst summarized the flaw in one line: by accelerating, the company focused on where it was selling and lost focus on what it was selling.

Three frameworks that explain the damage

A

Byron Sharp: mental and physical availability

mental and physical availability. Ehrenberg-Bass research argues brands grow by maximizing reach (penetration) through being easy to notice (mental availability) and easy to buy (physical availability). Nike voluntarily cut its physical availability. Competitors moved onto the shelf space it vacated. In the long run, the Double Jeopardy law says smaller-penetration brands also see slightly lower loyalty, so losing reach hurts twice.

B

The unit-economics trap

Suppose a shoe retails at $100, wholesales at $50, and costs $25 to make.

Wholesale contribution per pair = 50 − 25 = $25, with the partner supplying traffic, staff, and shelf.
DTC contribution per pair = 100 − 25 − (fulfilment + returns + store/digital costs + traffic acquisition).
Total = Σ_channel [ Q_c × (p_c − cogs − v_c) ] − F_c

DTC looks better per pair. But Q, the volume, is a function of traffic, and in wholesale the partner's footfall delivers that for free. Nike replaced borrowed traffic with bought traffic. The per-unit margin looked higher while the total quantity, and therefore the total profit, fell. The missing variable was Q.

C

Long-and-short marketing (Binet & Field)

(c) Long-and-short marketing (Binet & Field). Effective brands balance brand building (long-term, emotional, reach) with activation (short-term, sales-driven). One commentator describes the shift under Donahoe as a move from a data-driven, digital-first model to a more holistic, athlete-focused strategy under Hill. Read through this lens, the digital-first period overweighted activation and let brand-building atrophy. The evidence for that specific budget claim is thin, so treat it as an analytical reading, not a proven fact.

7. The turnaround: "Win Now" and "Sport Offense"

7. The turnaround: "Win Now" and "Sport Offense"

Elliott Hill, a Nike veteran, rejoined in 2024 as CEO to turn around sluggish sales, with a plan branded Win Now. In brand-theory terms, it is a repositioning back to the core identity, with four moves:

Rebuild the wholesale channel. Nike rebuilt relationships with partners like DSW and Macy's and announced in May 2025 its return to direct sales on Amazon. The numbers agree: in FY26, wholesale grew 4%, led by double-digit growth in North America.

Re-center sport. The Sport Offense reorganization aligns the business by sport rather than by gender or category. This is a shift in brand architecture logic: from demographic segmentation to occasion- and passion-based organization.

Renew performance innovation. The stated target is to reclaim ground from maximalist running competitors such as Hoka. In Keller's terms, this repairs the performance and judgments tiers of the pyramid.

Clean the marketplace. Reduce over-reliance on retro icons and clear legacy inventory to restore scarcity and heat.

The company is candid about the pace of repair. Hill said in his Q4 call that the results are not there yet. A new CFO, David Denton, takes over in mid-August 2026.

The takeaway

The key point is that a brand turnaround takes longer than a quarter. Any single data point is noise against the three-year arc of physical availability, product pipeline, and myth renewal.

Three formulas to memorize:

Brand value (Interbrand-style) ≈ Σ [ economic profit × role-of-brand index ] discounted at a rate set by brand strength
Royalty relief = PV of (revenue × royalty rate × (1 − tax))
Channel profit = Σ [ volume × (price − cost − variable cost) ] − fixed cost, where volume depends on who supplies the traffic

Common traps: confusing brand strength with brand value; treating DTC as automatically superior because unit margin is higher; and explaining a decline with one cause. Also don't forget that a brand's distinctive assets (Swoosh, Jumpman) have stayed intact throughout.
